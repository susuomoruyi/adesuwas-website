Notes on Building a Company

(or anything at all)

(or anything at all)

1. The honeymoon phase does not last forever

Listening to someone talk about their business idea is quite funny; their heads are always in the clouds. As far as they’re concerned, this business idea is going to be better than anything that has ever existed before. You should definitely feel this way about your business idea, by the way; it’s just funny.

I should also let you know that after this feeling, you will ask yourself a series of questions as you build your company: “Who sent me?” “I had a good job, what was I thinking?” “This company is definitely not going to make it.” “Am I even the best person to build whatever this is?”

2. A rule of thirds

The honeymoon phase not lasting forever isn’t bad news, in my opinion. There’s something called the rule of thirds: when you’re doing anything in life, like building a startup, you will feel very good one third of the time, okay one third of the time, and horrible one third of the time.

Bad days just show that you’re experiencing the very normal emotions of doing or building anything. While the honeymoon phase doesn’t last forever, you will experience it one third of the time — not just at the beginning when you’re ideating and counting your chickens before they hatch, but also while building. Sometimes after bad days, other times after okay days.

3. Don’t die before your startup

When we first started building ***Accrue, I used to feel very guilty about resting. When I tried to rest, I’d feel like I should be working and that the company would probably die because I was currently being lazy. Maybe the opportunity that would lead to our watershed moment was not being utilised because I was watching Netflix.

I struggled with this for a while until one of my friends told me this, very kindly, but without mincing words: “If your startup will die, it will die whether you take time to rest or not, but don’t die before your startup.”

Founders tend to be so immersed in what they’re building that they forget to have a life. I took my friend’s advice. I think you should too — don’t die before your startup.

4. Be a figure-outer

It was a shock to find out that a lot of people I admired years back for building amazing companies were just normal people like me who also didn’t know what they were doing. They were just figuring it out every day.

I remember how shocked I’d be after having conversations with some of them, because you could tell they were being very honest about the fact that they also really didn’t know. They just woke up every day and figured things out. They experimented with different ideas and different approaches. Some worked, a lot of them flopped, but they weren’t superstars in the way that I had pedestalised them. They were figure-outers.

It made me believe that I could be a figure-outer too, despite my not knowing.

5. Keep a gratitude journal

I have a gratitude journal, and at the end of every day, during my devotion, I write these words: “What am I grateful to God for today?” I then go ahead to list out everything I can think of that sparked joy that day.

If I had really delicious jollof, it would make my list. If nothing sparked joy, I’d tell God that I’m frustrated but grateful that I can dump my frustration on Him. I’d also thank Him that we were able to pay salaries at Accrue the previous month.

Here’s why I think this is important: startups are fast-paced, so you hardly get time to pause and catch your breath. The achievement of one goal immediately signals the start of the journey towards achieving a new one. If you’re not careful, you’ll lose sight of all the good you’ve achieved and constantly feel like you’ve not made progress because the moment you score a goal, the goalpost shifts again.

Keeping a gratitude journal helps me centre myself and remind myself that the things going well are worth pausing to be thankful for.

6. Take advice from investors with a pinch of salt

Investors invest in a lot of companies so they can maximise value. If your company fails, they’d be sad, but they can maximise value from some other company. You, on the other hand, have just one company.

So when someone who has options gives you advice, be careful to examine whether it actually works for you. You know your business better than your investors. If that were not the case, they wouldn’t invest money in it.

An investor may think it’s a good idea to splurge on a fancy TV ad because it will “attract customers.” You should be able to think for yourself and assess it based on your own reality and what you know about your business. If TV ads are too expensive and will just result in you burning cash, you can actually thank them for their advice and say no.

Don’t do everything an investor tells you about your business because you think they know better than you. They actually don’t.

7. Quickly learn to be a manager

When your company starts, chances are that you have maybe one or two other people on the team that you probably cannot even afford to pay. At this point, your business is pretty much a one-man business because everybody is doing the grunt work to get it from 0 to 1.

As your company grows, do not stay stuck in the sole proprietor mindset. Become a good manager.

Over the years, my mentor has helped me see that becoming a good manager isn’t something you can wing. We often like to believe we can freestyle soft skills and don’t need to learn. You cannot.

You have to read a lot, listen to good leadership material, learn from good leaders, and take courses. You’re not automatically a good manager because you have ten people working under you that you can give instructions to. Go and learn.

8. Growth is not linear

When you start growing your business, you will be tempted to set lofty goals. Don’t we all want to 10x revenue in two months? Lmao.

Some months, you’ll meet your month-on-month target. Other times, things may dip. Low months can be very discouraging, so always try to zoom out. How much was your company making this time last year? How much were you making a few months ago?

If you’re growing well when you zoom out, you will be fine.

Of course, keep setting your lofty goals and trying to reach them. But it’s okay when you don’t.

9. Always tell the truth

Because startups are incredibly hard, a lot of disappointing things may happen along the way. Like in Accrue’s case, you may be down to a few months of runway, with the company nearing its end, and have to lay off people or make huge pay cuts.

It’s hard to admit to people that you’re not doing as well as they think. It’s even harder to admit that you’re doing very badly. So you may be tempted to avoid investors and employees. You may want to lie and tell them things are fine when they’re not.

We like to fake it till we make it, but don’t do that.

When your company is about to die, and you must lay off people or cut salaries, tell your employees the truth. When you’ve used up investors’ money without achieving what you thought you would, tell your investors the truth.

A few years ago, we had to let someone go because we had run out of money to pay them. The conversation was incredibly hard, but we told the truth. When the company was finally in a better place, he was excited to join the team again because he knew he could trust us. And he knew this because we had always told him the truth.
