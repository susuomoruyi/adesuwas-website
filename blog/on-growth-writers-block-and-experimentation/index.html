On Growth, Writer’s Block, and Experimentation

A big part of why I’ve often felt stuck in my work is that I haven’t always known how to grow a customer base beyond a certain point. And if you’re building anything, chances are you’ve felt that too. You get to one level, and suddenly the path to the next level is not obvious at all.

That feeling of being stuck reminds me a lot of writer’s block.

I like writing, but there are weeks when I can’t seem to put pen to paper. And even when I do, whatever comes out feels weak or clumsy or not quite like what I meant to say. One thing that helps me every time is taking the pressure off and letting myself write badly first. I scribble rough thoughts. I stop trying to sound brilliant. I stop waiting for the perfect opening sentence to arrive.

I don’t think there’s a single cure for writer’s block.

Some people write only 150 words just to get going. Some write 100. Some write exactly the way they speak. Some tell a story first and find the point later. Some write vulnerably about the block itself. Some go somewhere quiet. Some go somewhere noisy where they can observe people and get out of their own heads. Most people do a combination of things until something unlocks.

That’s what makes it so interesting to me: the way out is rarely one magical solution. It’s usually a series of attempts.

I think growth feels very similar.

There’s a kind of mental block that happens when you don’t know how to push past a particular ceiling with your product. Maybe you’ve gotten your first 100 users, and getting to 500 now feels like a herculean task. Or maybe you’ve gotten your first 1,000, but the things that helped you get there suddenly seem insufficient for getting to 5,000.

That can be deeply frustrating, because there’s always the temptation to believe someone else has figured out the one thing you’re missing. The silver bullet. The growth tactic that works every time. The channel you haven’t discovered yet. The perfect message. The perfect campaign. The perfect strategy.

But I honestly don’t think that’s how it works.

Just like writer’s block, growth rarely opens up because of one perfect answer. It usually opens up because you try enough things, learn fast enough, and stay open long enough for something to click.

That’s why I keep coming back to experimentation.

My favourite thing about leading a marketing team is the adrenaline rush of that process. The brainstorming. The endless experiments. The fact that you never fully know which idea will work before you try it. The quick pivots when something clearly isn’t working. The excitement of seeing one experiment land beautifully. The humility of watching another fall flat. Then doing it all over again.

That, to me, is the work.

Not over-romanticising strategy. Not assuming growth will happen because you found one “best practice” online. But building a habit of testing, learning, and adjusting over and over again.

One practical way to approach this is to make experimentation a regular rhythm instead of something you do only when growth slows down.

  • At the end of every quarter, sit down and come up with a list of growth experiments you can run in the next one. Keep the list short enough to execute well, and simple enough that you won’t spend the entire quarter planning instead of testing. The point is not to make the list impressive. A few ideas you can actually ship will teach you more than a long strategy document full of things you never get around to trying.
  • Then measure them honestly. What worked? What didn’t? What showed promise but needs refinement? What completely missed the mark? Which experiment moved awareness, sign-ups, conversions, retention, referrals, or revenue? Which one only felt exciting internally but changed nothing externally? I wrote an article on how to measure the correct data for consistent growth. I think I could help you know how/what data to track to drive growth for your business. Double down on the experiments that are clearly working. Improve the ones that are close. Archive the ones that are not helping. Don’t drag dead ideas around just because they took effort to execute.
  • Then repeat the process again next quarter.

That repetition matters. Because experimentation is not a one-time fix for a hard season. It’s a way of operating. It’s how you keep finding your way forward even when the old playbook stops working.

I think a lot of people assume strong marketing teams are the ones with all the answers. I don’t think that’s true. I think the strongest teams are often the ones willing to test more, learn faster, and stay less emotionally attached to being right the first time.

So if you’re at that point where growth feels stuck, this is probably what I’d say: don’t spend all your energy searching for the silver bullet. Spend it building your next set of experiments.

That’s usually where the momentum is.

And because I know it helps to see what this looks like in practice, I’m sharing some experiments we’ve run at Accrue — I can’t write out everything, but I made it a long enough list, so it’s more helpful.

It’s not a formula or a guaranteed playbook, but it's a starting point. Steal the ones that make sense for your business. Adapt them. Test them. Keep what works. Let go of what doesn’t.
