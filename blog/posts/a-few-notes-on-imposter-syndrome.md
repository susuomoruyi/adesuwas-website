Don’t we all know a thing or two about imposter syndrome?

I was 21 when we first started talking about building [Accrue](https://useaccrue.com/). I hadn’t even graduated from university at the time because of COVID. I was already working as a product marketer at Helicarrier, but beyond that, I didn’t have much experience that made me feel like I could confidently start and grow a company.

Interestingly, the first emotion I felt wasn’t imposter syndrome; it was delusion.

In my mind, there were endless possibilities and no reason why I wouldn’t achieve them. As you can probably guess, the high from delusion eventually settles and gives way to imposter syndrome.

I’d often tell my friends then that I had absolutely no idea what I was doing, or if I was even the right person to do it. I was obviously not the most qualified person, so imposter syndrome really had me in a chokehold.

Having experienced a lot of imposter syndrome over the years, here are four notes I have on it:

**1. Imposter syndrome can be a good thing**

I’ve come to see imposter syndrome as a good thing. Early on, I saw it as a reminder that I was incompetent and couldn’t do what I set out to do. I now see it as a reminder that I’m not remaining stagnant. I’m stepping out of my comfort zone to do things that are bigger than me, so I’m growing.

Imposter syndrome often shows up beside ambition. People who never try anything bigger than themselves may not have to deal with it as often. It tends to show up when you care, when the stakes feel high, or when you’re doing something you’ve never done before.

**2. Your comfort zone expands the more you step out of it**

I also think imposter syndrome has a way of showing you what still feels big to you. There are things that once felt so far outside my range that I couldn’t even imagine them becoming normal. Certain goals, certain conversations, certain places, certain levels of responsibility. They felt huge because I had not lived with them long enough for them to feel familiar.

But the more you step into those things, the more your idea of “normal” starts to expand. That’s what I mean when I say your comfort zone expands. It’s not that the work suddenly becomes easy. It’s that you become more used to the weight of it. You stop being shocked by the scale. You stop treating every big thing like it is impossible.

I never thought it would be possible to get to 100k users for Accrue. At the time, when I had it as a goal, it felt so humongous, and that came with a lot of imposter syndrome. But now, that’s light work because we’re at about 400k customers.

And it’s not because 100k stopped being a big number. It’s because my sense of what was possible expanded.

**3. Confidence comes from data, not dopamine**

I heard someone say this on a podcast a few weeks ago and I rewound it a couple of times just to hear it again. It was so good. We tend to wait for a bout of confidence before we feel equipped to do something groundbreaking, but it doesn’t work like that. Confidence is built by data, not dopamine.

Think about babies. They feel confident that they can attempt walking because they crawled first. They feel confident that they can attempt running because they first took their first steps. If you want to build confidence to do really great stuff, you can’t wait for that confidence to descend on you through motivational speeches. You have to build confidence by doing.

Don’t wait for your imposter syndrome to fade away before you feel like you can do the work.

Do the work, and that gives your mind data to build confidence.

**4. Never underestimate your ability to figure things out**

Sometimes, the uncomfortable truth is that you are an imposter.

Maybe you don’t have the experience yet. Maybe you don’t have the answers yet. Maybe you’re in rooms, roles, or situations that feel bigger than you because, at that point, they actually are.

But you are not just an imposter. You are also someone who can learn, adapt, and figure things out.

Accrue will be five next year, and one thing I can say for sure is that this journey has required a lot of figuring out. I’ve had to keep learning and improving so I can become the kind of person capable of building the quality of business I want to build.

That has meant learning how to run a business, make our unit economics make sense, manage people, give teammates feedback more effectively, and make better decisions with imperfect information.

It has meant doing an MBA. It has meant getting a teacher to teach me French for two hours every day after our expansion into Francophone Africa last year.

And honestly, the list is endless. But the point is this: you have to show up every day, ten toes down, ready to figure things out. I cheekily love to call myself a “figure-outer” because when I look back on my journey, that is what so much of it has been.

Not always knowing, not always feeling ready, and not always being the most qualified person in the room, but being willing to learn fast enough, try hard enough, and stay with the problem long enough to become who the work requires me to be.

---

The more I do things that once scared me, the less scary they become. Not because I suddenly become fearless, but because I have evidence. I can look back and say, “I’ve done something like this before. I’ve figured things out before. I’ve survived this kind of uncertainty before.”
