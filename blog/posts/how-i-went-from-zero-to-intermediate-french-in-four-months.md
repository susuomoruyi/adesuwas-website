*For anyone who wants to learn French*

One Friday morning in March, I woke up and decided I was going to learn French. I still find it funny because it was not something I had been thinking about for a long time. The decision felt spontaneous. That same day, I found a French tutor, and shortly after, I began taking lessons.

In November last year, [Accrue](https://useaccrue.com/) expanded into Francophone Africa. Although I was not actively considering learning French at the time, I think that expansion may have influenced what felt like an impulsive decision a few months later. Accrue now has more than 11,000 customers in Cameroon, so my French will certainly come in handy.

I am writing this for anyone who would also like to learn French. I will share what has helped me, how I believe you can approach learning for better results, and the small — and not-so-small — things I have done over the past four months.

## Understand the French proficiency levels

French proficiency is commonly measured using six levels: A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, and C2.

**A1 is the beginner level.** At this stage, you can introduce yourself, ask and answer simple questions, and understand basic everyday expressions.

**A2 is the elementary level.** You can handle simple conversations about familiar topics such as work, family, shopping, and daily routines.

**B1 is the first intermediate level.** You can understand the main points of clear conversations, talk about your experiences and plans, and manage many situations while travelling in a French-speaking country.

**B2 is the upper-intermediate level.** You can hold longer conversations, explain your opinions clearly, and understand more detailed speech and writing.

**C1 is the advanced level.** You can communicate confidently and naturally in social, academic, and professional settings.

**C2 is the highest level.** At this stage, you can understand almost everything you hear or read and express yourself with a high level of accuracy and ease.

When I say I reached an intermediate level in four months, I mean that I progressed from having no knowledge of French to being able to understand and communicate beyond basic beginner conversations. These levels are useful because they give you clear milestones and make it easier to track your progress.

## 1. Get a French teacher

Do not rely on an app as your main way of learning French. You may learn how to say *oui* and *merci*, but you will probably struggle with sentence structure, forming your own simple and complex sentences, and understanding why certain expressions work the way they do.

Language apps are useful for building your vocabulary and practising what you already know, but they are not always the best at explaining the structure and rules of the French language. They should support your learning, not replace proper lessons.

When choosing a teacher, look for someone who has formally studied French, not simply someone who can speak it. This is important. For example, you may speak English fluently, but that does not necessarily mean you can teach it. There are probably many rules you use correctly every day without knowing how to explain them. Sometimes, you simply know that “this is how it is said.”

To teach a language effectively, a person needs to understand its grammar, structure, and rules well enough to explain them clearly.

Another reason an app cannot be your primary learning tool is that real learning needs to be immersive and detailed. For example, I take French classes for two hours every weekday, then spend another 45 minutes on homework and practice.

## 2. Change your phone and computer settings to French

This was the next thing I did after finding a tutor. Changing the language settings on my phone and computer exposed me to a lot of new vocabulary.

It created more opportunities to learn because I regularly came across unfamiliar words and expressions. Whenever I did, I looked up their meanings.

Immersion is one of the fastest ways to learn a language. Ideally, this would mean living in a place where the language is spoken every day. Since relocating may not be realistic, changing your device settings is a small but helpful step towards creating an immersive environment.

## 3. Watch French cartoons and vlogs on YouTube

Watching French cartoons, animations, and vlogs is another great way to become familiar with the language. It helps you hear how French speakers communicate in everyday life.

At first, you may not understand most of what is being said, and that is completely normal. Over time, you will begin to train your ears to recognise familiar words and sentence patterns. You will also pick up new phrases and expressions that you can start using yourself.

## 4. Speak bad French

One of the best ways to improve quickly is to start speaking as early and as often as possible.

Speak the imperfect French you know. Do not hold back because your pronunciation is not perfect or because you might use the wrong gender, article, or verb tense. The more you speak, the more easily you will notice your mistakes and learn how to correct them.

Think of yourself as a child learning to speak for the first time. Children do not wait until they can form perfect sentences before they start talking. They experiment, make mistakes, and improve through practice. Do not be embarrassed to babble your way through the learning process.

## 5. Visit a French-speaking country for a week or two

Before completing the A1 level, I visited the Republic of Benin for a week. The trip gave me an opportunity to practise what I had been learning in real-life situations.

I spoke a lot of imperfect French, but I made a genuine effort to express myself. The people I met were patient and often spoke slowly enough for me to follow the conversation.

Although the trip was short, it helped reinforce what I had been learning. It also gave me more confidence and showed me that I could communicate, even without speaking perfectly.

You do not need to wait until you are fluent before using the language. Start with what you know, create opportunities to practise, and allow yourself to improve one conversation at a time.
