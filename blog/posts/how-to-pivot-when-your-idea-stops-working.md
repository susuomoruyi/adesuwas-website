One of the best feelings in the world is having an idea you’re so excited about. It reminds me of when we first started [Accrue](https://useaccrue.com/) and how over the moon I was about the idea: an investment app that helps beginners invest in a way that limits their risk exposure. One of my employers at the time randomly mentioned to me that building a company was super hard. Lmao, I don’t think anything has ever gone in one ear and out the other as fast as those words did.

What do you mean, hard? That reality didn’t exist for me back then. As far as I was concerned, I was on the cusp of something extraordinary. Gosh, I can still feel that same excitement just thinking about it now.

Well, my employer was right. And I found out very quickly — building a company is very hard. For the sake of this piece, here’s how I’ll describe it.

It starts off like saying you want to travel from Lagos to Abuja by road. You know it’ll take about 12 hours, so you and your friends pile into an air-conditioned minivan and off you go. You’ve got all the snacks, you’ve downloaded your favourite shows, the weather forecast looks great, and everything points to a straightforward journey.

Two hours in, you hit unexpected traffic. At first you’re not worried, you assume it’ll clear up, but then you find out there’s been an accident up ahead and the road is completely blocked. Your carefully planned 12-hour journey is no longer going as planned.

Now you have two choices: stay on that road because it was the original plan, or look at what’s actually happening and figure out another way to get to Abuja.

Maybe you take a longer route. Maybe you turn around and try another road. Maybe, if it gets bad enough, you abandon the idea of driving altogether and find another way there.

But notice what hasn’t changed: you still want to get to Abuja.

The destination stayed the same. The route had to change.

And that’s what a pivot is.

When you start a business, your idea is essentially the route you believe will get you to your destination. You have a problem you want to solve, a customer you want to serve, and a way you believe you can build a profitable business around it.

But sometimes you get two hours into the journey and realise the road isn’t going where you thought it would.

The question then isn’t “How do I force this route to work?” It’s “What other route can get me where I want to go?”

Here are a few things that come with that:

## **1. Get over your emotional attachment, quickly**

I won’t say you shouldn’t be emotionally attached to your idea, because I don’t think that’s possible. But you should get over that attachment quickly and be more loyal to the destination: the survival and growth of your business.

When we started Accrue, the idea was to help people, especially beginners, invest in a way that protected them from risk. I was passionate about it because it was what we started with, what I was excited to build. But after a few months, the bear market hit, people got scared to invest in crypto and stocks, and they stopped using the product.

It was disappointing to let go of something I believed in so strongly, and scary too, because I didn’t know what would replace it. That’s the uncomfortable part of letting go of an idea: sometimes you have to abandon it before you know what’s coming next.

But we really wanted to survive, so we scrambled to figure out what was next.

## **2. Accept that your original assumptions may have been wrong**

A pivot requires being honest about what the data is telling you. Maybe customers don’t want the product the way you imagined it. Maybe they’re not willing to pay for it. Maybe the problem you thought was important simply isn’t important enough.

For us, the first sign was the bear market. We’d built around the assumption that people wanted to invest and that risk was what was stopping them. But the market changed, and suddenly people cared much more about protecting their money than putting it to work.

So we tried dollar savings. We figured if people were scared to invest, they might still want a safe way to save in dollars, earn interest, and protect what they had. But then we kept hearing something else, over and over: “I really like the app, but I don’t have money to put inside.”

That challenged another assumption. It wasn’t enough for people to like the product, they needed to actually have money to use it. And unless people are paying you simply because they like your app, you don’t have a business.

## **3. Go looking for evidence of where the new path might be**

Once you accept the original route isn’t working, you still don’t know what the new route is. You have to go looking for clues.

We got much more deliberate about this over time. Instead of sitting in a room trying to dream up the next brilliant idea, we started paying much closer attention to what people were actually doing with the product.

A woman had used the app to send money from Ghana to her aunt in Nigeria.

I remember thinking, wait, you can use the app to do this?

We were already scrambling to figure out what came next, so we latched onto this too and assumed there were other people like her who wanted to do the same thing.

## **4. Be willing to experiment**

Finding a potential new path doesn’t mean you know it’s the right one. You have to be willing to try things.

That’s essentially what we did each time we changed direction. We went from investing to dollar savings, then started exploring payments. Each move was a hypothesis about what might work, not some grand revelation that we knew for certain would succeed.

The important thing is you don’t need absolute certainty before you move. You can test, observe what happens, learn, and adjust again. It’s okay to look confused.

This is where I think the word “pivot” can be misleading. It makes it sound like there’s one dramatic moment where you turn the entire company around and suddenly know exactly where you’re going. In reality, it looks a lot more like trying one thing, learning something, trying another thing, learning something else, and gradually getting closer to a path that works.

## **5. Understand that a pivot may mean leaving something behind**

The other hard part of pivoting is accepting that not everything you built gets to come with you.

When we moved away from the original investing product, we left behind something we’d spent months building, something I was personally very excited about. When we moved away from dollar savings, we again left behind a product people liked, because it wasn’t solving a big enough problem for enough people.

That’s painful, because you naturally want to make the previous work count. You think, we’ve already spent all this time building this, surely there’s a way to make it work.

But sometimes there isn’t.

The fact that you’ve already driven three hours down a particular road doesn’t mean you should spend another eight hours on it just because you’ve come this far. The work isn’t necessarily wasted, but you may have to accept that what you built isn’t what gets you to your destination.

In our case, the opportunity that eventually opened up in intra-Africa payments became much bigger than anything we’d originally imagined. In about six to seven months, it went from roughly 4% of our revenue to over 70%.

---

Accrue has had to pivot a few times, and who knows, another one might be waiting for us in the future. But so far, this is what’s helped us navigate every one of them.
