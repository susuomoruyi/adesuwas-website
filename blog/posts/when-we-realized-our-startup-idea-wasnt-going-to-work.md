In September 2021, we launched the beta version of [Accrue](https://useaccrue.com/) — a dollar-cost averaging app for investing in stocks and crypto.

The beta launch was actually very successful. By January 26, 2022, we’d launched properly, and things were going great. People were catching on, we acquired our first thousand users in a short time, and revenue was growing month on month. Everything looked good.

Then the bear market hit.

## **The Beginning**

Let me backtrack a bit.

The reason we even built a dollar-cost averaging app in the first place was simple — a lot of people wanted to get into crypto and stocks, but they didn’t know how. When do I buy? What do I buy? How much should I put in?

We wanted to make that process easier. With Accrue, you could pick a quality asset like Bitcoin and invest a small amount consistently — maybe $1 a day, $10 a week, or $50 a month. It spreads your risk, takes away the pressure of timing the market, and helps you stay invested long-term.

It made sense. And for a while, it worked.

But when the bear market came, people stopped investing. Everything tanked — signups, deposits, revenue. All the growth we were seeing just vanished.

We were also talking to investors at the time, and as soon as the market turned, that interest disappeared too. It was confusing, scary, and honestly, very disappointing. One minute, it felt like we were on this beautiful trajectory — the next, it felt like it wasn’t going to work.

## **Trying to Save the Company**

We started talking to users, trying to figure out what to do so the company wouldn’t die. That’s how we ended up with our first pivot — dollar savings.

We thought, okay, if people don’t want to invest right now because of the market, maybe they just want to save. The dollar is stable, so let’s make it easy for people to save in dollars.

It made sense. It even sounded exciting. We launched it, people liked it, and it started picking up slowly.

But then, we began getting this kind of feedback on Twitter that sounded nice on the surface. Things like:

> “I love the Accrue app. The interface is beautiful. I just wish I had money to put inside.”

At first, it felt like a compliment. Our app was beautiful. But after hearing that kind of feedback multiple times, it hit us: unless people are paying us for having a beautiful app, we don’t really have a business.

That was hard to admit. It meant that even this new idea we’d pivoted to wasn’t working either.

## **The Second Realization**

Around this time, we were running out of runway and had to make some tough calls just to stay alive.

Then one day, something interesting happened. A user in Ghana used one of our features — originally built to help Ghanaians deposit seamlessly — to send money to her aunt in Nigeria. That wasn’t what we built it for, so it caught me off guard.

But it also got us thinking: if one person could use it that way, maybe others wanted to do the same.

That moment became the seed for our next pivot. Over the next few months, we focused on that feature, repurposed it for cross-border payments, and started spreading the word.

Within about seven months, it grew from just 3–4% of our revenue to over 70%. That was when things finally started to make sense again.

## **What I’m Learning**

- **You have to be willing to let go.**
When you build something, it’s easy to get attached to it. But sometimes, your first idea won’t work, and that’s okay. The faster you accept that, the faster you can find what will.
- **Listen carefully — even to what users don’t say.**
The people who said “I love the app but don’t have money to save” didn’t realize they were telling us the product wasn’t useful to them at that time. Sometimes feedback isn’t direct; you have to read between the lines.
- **Follow the data.**
The Ghanaian user who sent money to her aunt? That single action revealed a whole new direction for us. If we hadn’t been paying attention, we might have missed it.
- **Don’t be afraid to look like you’re figuring things out.**
Pivoting can make you feel like you’re all over the place, but it’s part of the process. Clarity comes from trying, so be the person who tries.

Looking back now, every “failure” taught us something. And future failures will continue to teach us valuable lessons. Each idea that didn’t work got us closer to the ones that did. Sometimes, that’s just how building goes — you keep trying, keep listening, keep adjusting until something finally clicks.

My friend, Feranmi, always says that there’s no silver bullet. You just have to keep trying things — that’s the annoying part, but it’s also the exciting part, lol.
