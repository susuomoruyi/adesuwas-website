Some Experiments That Helped Us Grow

In my last article, I wrote about how growth rarely comes from one magic answer.

I think people often assume there is a silver bullet somewhere. One campaign. One channel. One idea. One hire. One feature. One ad. One viral post. One partnership. One secret that successful companies know and everyone else is missing.

But in my experience, growth is usually much less dramatic than that.

It is mostly experimentation.

You try things. Many of them do not work. Some work a little. A few work really well. Then the market changes, customer behaviour changes, your product changes, and you start experimenting again.

That has been true for us at Accrue, too.

We have run many experiments over the years. A lot of them failed. That is the nature of experimentation. But some of them worked well enough to help us unlock growth, improve retention, increase usage, or bring in new users at little to no cost.

1. A calculator that helped people “time travel”

Accrue started as a dollar-cost averaging app.

Dollar-cost averaging is a simple investment approach where you invest a fixed amount consistently over time instead of trying to guess the perfect time to buy. So instead of waiting and wondering whether today is the best day to buy Bitcoin, for example, you might invest $5 every week, no matter what the market is doing.

One of the early experiments we ran was a dollar-cost averaging calculator.

It let people enter a scenario like: If I had invested $5 in Bitcoin every week since 2017, how much would I have invested, and how much profit would I have made?

What made it work was not just the calculator itself, but the emotion behind it. It gave people a way to imagine a different financial outcome for themselves. It let them “time travel” a little. People could immediately see what consistency might have done for them.

We also made it easy for people to share their results on Twitter, and the shared post invited other people to check their own results too. That created a viral loop. People posted theirs, their followers got curious, and more people came to try it.

That experiment got us our first 1,000 signups with zero ad spend.

That was one of the earliest reminders for me that sometimes growth is not about shouting louder. Sometimes it is about building something people naturally want to talk about.

2. “Invest like…”

Another experiment that worked for us was what we called the Invest Like feature.

The idea was simple: we reached out to well-known people in tech and fintech and asked them what assets they were investing in. Then we turned that into a feature inside the app. So instead of just seeing a list of assets, users could see something like Invest like X and explore that person’s allocation.

This worked because people are aspirational. They are often curious about how people they admire think, what they are paying attention to, and what decisions they are making.

And when you make that actionable, not just interesting, it becomes even more compelling.

People could also share who they were “investing like” on Twitter, which again created conversation and brought more attention to the product.

The deeper lesson here was that people do not always want information in the abstract. Sometimes they want context. Sometimes they want a human reference point.

3. Showing the best-performing assets every week

When we were still focused on the dollar-cost averaging product, one simple thing we did every week was highlight the top-performing assets from the previous week.

We were not necessarily telling people to buy them. We were simply surfacing what had done well.

That worked because beginner investors are often looking for guidance, even when they do not call it that. They want help narrowing the field. They want signals. They want something that helps them decide where to pay attention.

We noticed that whenever we shared those weekly winners, people tended to buy more.

It cost us nothing to do, but it helped drive activity because it matched a real customer behaviour: people are more likely to act when they feel they have a starting point.

4. Turning savings into competition

When the 2022 bear market hit, we pivoted away from the earlier product and moved into dollar savings.

One of the experiments that helped that product in its early days was savings competitions.

We built landing pages around two popular people and turned saving into a team-based competition. Users could join the team of the person they liked more and save as part of that team.

It felt a bit like a football rivalry, but for money.

That made saving feel less solitary and more social. It gave people something to root for. It created conversation on Twitter. It made people want to participate not just because of the financial outcome, but because of the identity and energy around it.

Sometimes the thing that gets people to act is not utility alone. Sometimes it is belonging. Sometimes it is play.

5. Weekend Happy Hour

At some point, we noticed that weekend usage dropped sharply. I think it was around 70%.

That made sense. People were resting, going out, doing other things. But even when something is understandable, it does not mean you should ignore it.

So we introduced what we called Weekend Happy Hour.

For one hour every weekend, users could transact on the app without some of the fees we would normally charge.

That single change gave people a reason to return during a period that was usually quiet. It increased weekend usage, but we still structured it in a way that did not damage our unit economics.

I like this experiment because it is a reminder that sometimes growth is not only about acquiring more users. Sometimes it is also about recovering lost moments in user behaviour.

6. Friday quizzes

One experiment I still really like is our Friday quizzes.

We built a quiz tool in-house and used it to ask money-related questions every Friday. Nothing too heavy. Just ten multiple-choice questions that let people test how much they knew about money.

At the end, people got a score and could share it on Twitter. People are competitive. They like showing off when they do well. So when someone scored 9/10 or 10/10, they would often share their result. And the shared preview included a link that invited other people to try the quiz too.

Every Friday, we started seeing signups increase by about 30%.

It also helped us get more followers and more visibility, because the shared links included our handle.

What I love about this one is that it is cheap, repeatable, and fun. It gave people a reason to engage with the brand every week without making that engagement feel like work.

7. Rewarding usage with coins

Another experiment that helped with retention was Crew coins.

The idea was straightforward: as users performed different actions on the app, they earned coins. Those coins could then be used to offset fees.

So the more you used the product, the more affordable it became to keep using it.

This worked because it aligned user behaviour with product value. It gave people a visible reason to come back. It made usage feel rewarding. And it made the relationship between action and benefit very clear.

Some experiments help you acquire users. Others help you keep them. This was more on the retention side.

8. Speaking to people in their own language

One experiment that really stood out for us was creating videos and ads in local dialects.

Most brands default to English for everything. We did too, for a while. But then we started making videos in Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Twi, and French for Francophone users.

Some of those performed really well.

I remember the first Twi ad especially. It passed a million views, which at the time was the highest views we had seen on any video.

What made it work was not just novelty. It felt familiar to people. Endearing, even. There is something powerful about hearing a product explained in a language that feels like home.

9. A Wall of Love that included the bad reviews too

We also built something we called the Wall of Love.

After users completed a transaction, we asked them to leave a comment about how it went. We then featured those reviews on a landing page people could browse.

What made this especially useful was that we did not only show the glowing reviews. We left the critical ones there too.

That honesty mattered. People are usually smart enough to tell when something feels too polished. Showing only perfect reviews can make a brand feel less trustworthy, not more. But showing the good, the bad, and the real creates credibility.

We also noticed that once people had articulated their thoughts in a review, some of them would go on to share the same experience publicly on social media.

So it was not just social proof. It also became a trigger for word of mouth.

10. Agent stories

When we started working with agents for our cross-border payments product, recruiting agents was not easy.

What helped was telling stories about the agents we already had.

For example, if an agent was making up to $300 monthly, we would write about it: how they got started, what the work looked like, what that extra income meant for them.

Every time we published stories like that, we saw an influx of people signing up to become agents.

That makes sense in hindsight. People are far more likely to imagine themselves doing something when they can see someone like them already doing it.

It was a reminder that sometimes you do not need to “sell the opportunity” harder. You just need to make it more visible and more believable.

11. Showing the team behind the brand

There was a period when most of our social media content was just product, product, product.

Over time, we started sharing more pictures and videos of the team. Team moments at events. Team gatherings. Behind-the-scenes clips. Small slices of the humans behind the company.

Some of those posts ended up becoming our highest-engagement posts.

I think it is because they humanized the brand.

People do not always want to interact with a logo. They want to feel like there are real people building the thing they use. They want to see some warmth. Some personality. Some life.

This is one of those experiments that sounds obvious after the fact, but a lot of us still underuse it.

12. Using a loss leader

Another thing that helped us was having a loss leader product.

A loss leader is a product or offer that may not make you much money directly, but attracts customers you can later convert into other, more profitable products.

For us, dollar savings played that role.

Saving in dollars on the app was not our biggest money-maker, but it was a strong acquisition hook. When people heard they could earn up to 6% interest on dollar savings, it got their attention. They signed up for that reason, and from there we had the opportunity to introduce them to other services on the app.

Not every feature has to be optimized for direct revenue from day one. Some can be optimized for entry.

13. Deep links instead of generic links

At first, when we sent out emails or promotions, the CTA would usually lead to our generic website page.

Later, we started using deep links.

So instead of clicking a button and landing on a homepage, a user who already had the app could go straight to the exact action we wanted them to take. If the email was about creating a virtual card, the button opened the virtual card page directly.

That reduced friction and improved conversion.

Many growth experiments sound exciting because they are public-facing. But sometimes the most useful wins come from removing one extra step in the user journey.

14. A Christmas tree campaign

One of the experiments we ran during Christmas was to give users in-app Christmas trees they could decorate.

To unlock ornaments, users had to complete profitable transactions. Depending on what they did, they could earn lights, garlands, baubles, snowflakes, and other decorations for their tree.

They could also share their decorated trees on Twitter.

What this did was turn ordinary product usage into a seasonal game. It gave people a visual sense of progress and something worth showing off.

This was a good reminder that even in financial products, play can be useful when it is thoughtfully designed.

15. Growing Instagram during KYC wait time

At some point, we were struggling to grow our Instagram account.

Then someone on the team suggested something simple: while users were waiting a few minutes for KYC verification, we could show them a message that said, in effect, We’re verifying your KYC. In the meantime, follow us on Instagram.

That worked surprisingly well.

It made use of a moment that would otherwise have been empty. Instead of treating the wait as dead time, we turned it into a small growth opportunity.

I like this example because it shows how experimentation is not always about inventing something big. Sometimes it is just about noticing where attention already exists and using it better.
